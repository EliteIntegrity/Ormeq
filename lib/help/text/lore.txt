How the Dungeons Got Here

Nobody built this place. It was collected.

The Collection

Look at the country you are standing in: roads, weather, seven towns, 
farmland going quietly to seed. Ordinary ground. None of it is the 
point.

The point is the holes in it. Nobody has ever agreed how many.

In this country, each of the great holes is a place that was taken from 
somewhere else and set down here. A court. A drowned city. A mountain 
with something asleep beneath it. A heaven, and separately a hell. 
These places are not piled up and they are not related to one another. 
They are laid out, the way a collection is laid out, in whatever order 
suited whoever put them there.

And each is far larger inside than the ground it covers. That is why 
they are holes and not counties: there is no room on the surface for a 
country, so the country goes downwards.

Deeper Means Further In

Depth here is not distance below your feet. It is distance into the 
thing you have entered.

The first floors of anywhere are its edges: outskirts, doorways, 
whatever happened to be nearest the walls when it was lifted. Go 
further and you come towards whatever that place kept at its heart, 
which is usually whatever its people were most afraid of. That is why 
deeper is worse. It has nothing to do with rock.

It is also why the numbers refuse to compare. Being ninety floors into 
a hell tells you nothing whatever about how far ninety floors would 
take you into a mine. They are separate objects, and they do not even 
mean the same thing by depth.

Where It Stops

At the outer edges of this country you come to ground you cannot cross. 
Not mountains. Not sea. It simply stops being ground.

This is what people find hardest to accept, and there is no comfort in 
the explanation: things were set down here. Nobody laid a road between 
them, because a road is something you build between places you meant to 
connect.

There were lands beyond it. That much is not in doubt -- there are 
roads on this side that were built to go somewhere, and they end in the 
middle of a field, and there are people still alive whose grandparents 
walked them. Whether those countries were taken, or are simply standing 
there on the other side of a gap that cannot be crossed, nobody knows. 
Nobody has ever come back across, in either direction.

So there is no reason to think this is the only such patch of ground. 
There is only no way to find out.

A washed-out road is a different sort of trouble. People still mend 
local bridges and find paths around floods. Losing a supply route this 
winter is not the same as losing contact with the lands beyond. One can 
happen long after the other, and putting the bridge back does not put 
the world back.

Who Was Here First

Somebody farmed this. You can see it: the field boundaries are older 
than any of the towns, and they were laid out by people who expected to 
stay.

They are still here. They are the yeeks, and this is their country.

Everybody finds this funny. Yeeks are small, weak, poor at nearly 
everything, and prone to screaming; the incomers use the word as an 
insult and have done for so long that most of them have forgotten it is 
a people. They are also the only people in this country whose history 
began here. Everyone else's history is somewhere underneath you.

And they have kept at it. They have had a king, and after the king a 
president, which is more constitutional argument than the rest of the 
towns have managed between them. It is treated as a joke. It is not 
obvious why.

If you want to know anything true about what takes the worlds, they are 
the only ones who were here before it started. They will not tell you 
much. Given the last thousand years, that seems fair.

The Name of the Country

Everyone calls this place Ammerow. Every town, every road sign, every 
drunk who cannot remember the way home.

Nobody can tell you what it means, and almost nobody has ever asked.

It is the yeeks' word for themselves. Not "yeek" -- that is what 
incomers named them, after the noise. Ammerow is what they have always 
called their own people, and the newcomers took it for the name of the 
ground, wore the edges off it, and have been saying it every day since 
without once noticing whose word it is.

The Towns

Almost nobody in the towns is from here.

Every so often a taken place comes to rest shallow enough that some of 
its people climb out. Most keep walking. The ones who stay build on 
whatever ground is going, sell what they carried, and learn the local 
words for things. It is why the smith stocks weapons nobody can name, 
why the shelves hold prayer books from four religions that never met, 
and why the queue outside the general store is a dozen kinds of person 
who all agree, more or less, on the price of rope.

Their children are from here, and from nowhere, which is a different 
thing and takes about a generation to sting.

Why the Great Things Are One of a Kind

When a place is taken, everything in it comes too, and nothing more is 
ever made. So the extraordinary sword you drag out of the sixtieth 
floor is not rare. It is the last one. The hands that forged it, the 
ore it was cut from and the country that needed it are all somewhere 
underneath you, and there will not be another.

Why He Comes

He does not search. He has never needed to.

Every place in this collection is a place where somebody said his name.

That is the whole of it. Not a curse, not a bargain, nothing you could 
sign. A name said aloud is an address, and he is very good at 
addresses. Somewhere in each of these holes is the room where it 
happened, and usually you can tell which one it was.

So it is not said here. Not in the towns, not on the roads, not as a 
joke, and not in this document either.

What people say instead is Ormel.

That is not another word for him. It is his name with the last sound 
taken off, and everyone knows it. The rule children are taught is 
simple enough to keep under pressure: you may say the name as far as 
the end, and the end is the address. Start it and turn aside, and you 
are safe. Finish it and you have written to him.

So the towns say Ormel a dozen times a day, with no more weight than 
any other name, and every one of them is a flinch. You will get used to 
it faster than you expect. Everyone does.

That is not superstition. Superstition is when the ritual has outlived 
the reason.

What People Do About It

If somebody says it indoors -- a drunk, a child, a newcomer nobody 
thought to warn -- everyone goes outside. The one who said it stays in 
and puts out the fire, and is not spoken to until morning. Then the 
door is opened from the outside and somebody asks them, by name, to 
come out.

Nobody can tell you whether it works. It has not stopped working yet, 
which is the only test anyone has.

The One With No Bottom

All but one of the holes are places. One is not.

It has no theme, no floor anybody has found, and nothing in it that 
came from anywhere in particular. It is the only one that was never 
taken from somebody, because it is not part of the collection.

It is where the collector lives.

The way in is known and unguarded, which tells you what he thinks of 
the risk. Go down far enough and you will meet him, and at the very end 
you will find out what he is actually called -- a thing worth knowing 
once and no earlier, because up here it is the only word that has ever 
cost a world.
